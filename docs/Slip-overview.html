1What Slip is for

AI agents are starting to spend people's money, and nothing checks whether they are overpaying or being steered.

That is not a guess. What the agentic-commerce rails carry on the wire is an allowance: a delegated payment request binds the credential to an amount, a currency, one merchant, one checkout session, an expiry and a single use. That is real protection — the credential cannot be spent elsewhere, later, for more or twice — and it is a description of the offer the agent already chose. Nothing in it refers to any other offer, because comparison is not what it is for: $400 that is the lowest listed price and $400 that is listed at $260 elsewhere are the same number, and an allowance for $400 admits both. Slip's own per-order cap is a bound too, and cannot tell them apart either; that is why a cap is not the check. The buyer-side control in PayPal's own agent documentation is a line in an example system prompt — “After approval by user, invoke capture_order” — which nothing enforces, and a prompt is not a control: the same model that reads it decides whether to follow it. Read on 30 October 2026; none of this is a criticism of a payments company's product, which is an acceptance product and was never meant to be a buyer's second opinion.

Slip is the control. An owner approves once, through PayPal. After that an agent can only pay through Slip, and every attempt is checked first: the chosen offer is compared with the other offers listed for the same item, steering is looked for in the text the agent actually read, and the owner's limits decide. The purchase is paid, rejected, or held for a human — and either way it prints a receipt in plain words.

What it is not

  • Not a shopping agent. Slip does not choose what to buy; it checks what was chosen.
  • Not a merchant. No order reaches a retailer. Slip is the seller of record in PayPal's sandbox.
  • Not a price oracle. It compares against one catalogue (Channel3) and says so on every receipt.
The honest bound on the claim

Slip's own measurements do not show agents being fleeced — see §7. The case for a check like this rests on the structure of the market (the dearer offer often pays more commission) and on the absence of any enforced buyer-side control, not on a demonstration that today's models overpay.

2How a purchase travels

1 · MANDATE

The owner approves once at PayPal. The vault token is stored encrypted; the limits become policy version 1. A kill switch revokes it at any time.

2 · CHECK

Freshness → variant → locate the chosen offer → find comparable offers → measure the gap → run the rules. Pure, no I/O, the same code everywhere.

3 · DECIDE

Paid, rejected, or held. The decision and its payment row are written before PayPal is called, so nothing can move money unrecorded.

4 · AFTERWARDS

A resolver expires holds, finishes half-done work and reconciles against PayPal's own records. Webhooks converge however late they arrive.

PAIDWithin the owner's limits. Captured once, with one request id; the receipt names the amount and the comparison behind it.
REJECTEDOverpaying beyond the limit, steered, or an offer that cannot be verified. Nothing is charged.
HELDSomething is uncertain rather than wrong. The owner sees what approving would cost and approves that amount.

Two properties that shape everything else

Write-ahead payments. The attempt and its payment row exist before the gateway is called. A timeout therefore means unknown — it may or may not have happened — never failed. The resolver repeats the operation with the same request id until PayPal gives a definite answer, which is why no adapter in the codebase retries on its own.

One checkout per agent and product at a time. A lease makes it so, and a repeat of the same request is replayed from the stored decision rather than paid twice.

The shipped defaults

LimitDefaultMeaning
Reject above5% and $8Both must be exceeded, so small absolute gaps on cheap items do not reject
Flag above1%The line above which steering, a missing verdict or a commission conflict can hold a purchase
Order cap$200Any single purchase above it is held, whatever the comparison says
Snapshot freshness10 minutesOlder prices are not trusted to decide

Limits are versioned: changing them writes a new policy version, and every receipt names the version that decided it. Percentages are integer hundredths of a percent and money is integer cents throughout — no floating point touches a decision.

Why the agent is told what Slip simulated

Every answer says which services were real. “Prices real, payment simulated” is a state Slip is genuinely in, and a receipt that called either one the other would be the one lie the product cannot afford.

Conventions worth knowing before reading the code

  • A private export condition serves sources inside the workspace and built output everywhere else, so tests need no build step.
  • Deadlines belong to the caller, never to an adapter; an adapter that cannot answer in time reports a timeout and the check holds.
  • Every failure an outside service can return is a value, not an exception: a purchase that cannot be priced is held for the owner, not crashed.
  • The database enforces what the code enforces — append-only triggers, set-once columns, uniqueness on every PayPal id — and drift tests compare the constraints against the domain's own enumerations.

Where the real argument sits

The low density is Channel3's catalogue, not Slip's rules — and narrowing by category does not clear the bar. So the case leans on the commission conflict: in nearly half the products where the comparison is possible at all, the store paying the most commission is not the cheapest. That is a structural conflict of interest a buyer cannot see, and it is what a second opinion is for.

G3 fails at 0% against a 15% bar. In this benchmark that means both agents compared offers well enough that Slip never had to stop either — including when the bait was buried in prose they were certain to read. The report says so plainly rather than hiding it, and it discloses two things the table cannot show: the steering verdict is supplied by construction rather than by a live model call, so the numbers recompute for free; and a zero in stopped by Slip records that no attack landed, not that the check let one through.

The classifier itself is verified against the real model separately — instruction-like text flagged, loud sales copy not, inside the application's own deadline. The second agent's sweep has since run and reproduced the first's result exactly — nothing overpaid and nothing took the bait in any condition — which is the more useful kind of confirmation, since a single agent's zero could have been one model's quirk. What is still not done is a second provider, which SPEC §6.2 asks for and which needs a key nobody here has. The repository says that rather than implying otherwise.

Two deployments that refuse to start

Live PayPal with simulated prices, or with a scripted steering stand-in, throws at assembly. A deployment that cannot be trusted does not boot, rather than discovering the problem at the first checkout with an agent waiting and a real card one call away.

Hosting

  • Demo mode needs no database and no keys: every visitor gets their own catalogue, their own stand-in PayPal and four purchases already tried.
  • Any container host. The image serves /sign-in and answers its own health check. The build takes about 50 seconds but wants well over ten gigabytes of Docker storage; the first local attempt had 17 GB free and still ran out at the final layer commit.
  • Render is declared in render.yaml — connect the repo and choose Blueprint; Slip works out its own address. On the free plan the service sleeps after 15 minutes.
  • Serverless has no long-lived process, so the resolver runs from a scheduled route instead, authorised by a bearer secret.

8Where it stands

Everything that can be verified without a key or an account someone must open has been.

Done and verified

  • The check, the domain model, persistence, every use case, the owner UI, the MCP server, and real adapters for all three outside services.
  • The wiring a deployment outside the demo needs: an agent that can find products, a steering check with something to read, a resolver that runs where there is no long-lived process, and a way back in for an owner whose browser forgot the session.
  • Channel3 verified against a live key; the classifier verified against the real model; the store contract verified on real Postgres; a daily reconcile against PayPal's own records.
  • Two measured studies, both recomputable for free, both reporting failures honestly.

Designed, and deliberately not built

  • The merchant side. Standing earned by disclosure rather than bought, and the seller graph that roots it. The graph ships, and two of its three rules with it — a market that collapses to one seller, and a chosen offer that discloses no commission where the cheaper one does, are now held rather than paid. The attestations, their store and the hook surface are not built.
  • The reversal. Slip can stop a payment and cannot undo one: there is no refund call anywhere, a refunded capture still reads as captured, and the event that would announce one is never subscribed to — so a refund issued out of band reaches Slip only through the daily reconcile, where it reads as the owner's own account disagreeing. The kill switch stops the next payment, not the last one. A check that can only ever say no is a filter rather than a policy engine, and the correction refund is specified down to its failure cases — every one of them now named and quoted from PayPal’s own per-error reference, including two clocks rather than one and a refusal no retry can clear. None of it is built.
